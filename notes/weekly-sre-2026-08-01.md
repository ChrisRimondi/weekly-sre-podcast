The diverse root causes—ranging from cooling failures to regional network boundary disruptions—highlight the layered nature of cloud reliability, and reinforce why multi-region design and rapid escalation paths are no longer optional for services with stringent SLOs.

Meanwhile, lengthy downtime from providers like Snowflake, Akamai, and Azure Entra exposes how failures in identity, CDN, and data warehouse components can cascade downstream, amplifying user-facing outages. Let’s get started.

## Highlights

This week has showcased just how multifaceted and interconnected reliability concerns are in large-scale production systems. The AWS outage in the US-West-2 region stands out as a clear illustration of how issues at the boundaries of internet connectivity can cascade into broad service disruptions. Critically, it was the third major outage in less than three months, with root causes spanning hardware, external dependencies, and now regional boundary routing. For SREs, this recurrence across independent layers highlights not only the importance of layered defense-in-depth strategies, but also keeping a sharp focus on single-region dependencies. Multi-region or even multi-cloud deployments remain essential for organizations with high uptime requirements, but they bring their own complexities: think about data consistency, network ingress/egress costs, and operational overhead. Still, with the frequency of these region-wide incidents, the cost-benefit calculus shifts toward greater geographic redundancy and improved failover automation. 

The sheer diversity of recently impacted services—ranging from financial platforms like Apple Pay to social and streaming giants—serves as a tactical reminder: your SLOs must be informed by the weakest links in your upstream dependencies. During the AWS event, organizations that had runbooks ready for this class of incident and that had cross-region failover pre-tested were able to maintain user trust and continuity far better. A notable lesson for production teams is to revisit their dependencies’ past incident logs as part of threat modeling and to explicitly include single-region hard dependencies as an SLO risk factor.

Certificate lifecycle management surfaced as a recurring and preventable cause of outage, as highlighted by the GitHub Actions runner disruption due to an expired internal SSL certificate. Here, the technical breakdown is simple but revealing: Actions runners, widely used in CI/CD pipelines, rely on secured API endpoints for command and control. The lapse of a certificate, often because of opaque internal automation or missed reminders, led to a hard outage for runners attempting to connect. For engineering organizations, this underlines the need for both process and tooling maturity: automated certificate renewal (e.g., integrated with your configuration management or secrets rotation tools), inventory systems for certificate tracking, and alerting on upcoming expirations—ideally, warnings weeks ahead of expiry. Some teams find value in quarterly or even monthly certificate reviews integrated into operational readiness reviews. SREs should also consider the complexity of certificate chains and validation; monitoring chain validity, not just the leaf certificate, can catch a variety of subtle misconfigurations before they bite you.

On the authentication and identity service front, both Supabase and Microsoft Azure suffered significant incidents. Supabase’s outage, while relatively brief at 41 minutes, nonetheless caused user access failures—a hard outage from a user journey perspective. Azure’s Identity (Entra) event was even more severe, nearly 11 hours, underlining how essential identity services are to the entire cloud stack. For incident response, these cases show the absolute necessity for clearly defined SLIs on authentication APIs, prioritized alerting for auth failures, and resilient architecture choices—for example, cross-region hot spares, circuit-breaker mechanisms to gracefully degrade when primary services are unavailable, and fallback to cached or federated auth paths when possible.

The GitHub Issues and Pull Requests service disruption—eight hours plus—hits on a core SRE teaching: reliability is about more than just raw uptime; it’s also about velocity and productivity. For software teams, collaborative tooling outages don’t just block deploys; they erode developer trust and can have knock-on effects for feature delivery schedules. SREs supporting internal tooling benefit from applying the same rigor and playbook discipline as they do with external-facing services: that means deep monitoring, synthetic checks, and proactive communications. There’s mounting evidence that clear, transparent user communications (think well-updated status pages and in-product alerts) reduce long-term brand damage in a way that scrambling for silent fixes never does.

Content delivery reliability remains a recurring theme, with Akamai’s multi-minute CDN outage this week and new product moves from Cloudflare. With CDNs cemented as the backbone of latency-sensitive applications, SREs need to deeply understand their caching, invalidation, and failover behaviors. A single CDN outage can spike origin load, so dual-provider or multi-CDN architectures deserve attention, especially now that major platforms like Cloudflare are offering orchestrated support for these patterns. Proper health checks, circuit-breaking at the edge, and careful route management become critical. Plan for the unexpected: distributed failure tests (e.g., traffic shifting or DNS-based provider failover) should be part of the routine chaos toolkit.

Snowflake’s extended data warehousing outage demonstrates that even specialized backend-as-a-service offerings require careful reliability review. Seven hours offline exposes fragility not just in the operations team’s incident handling, but also in failover planning and communications to customers. If your architecture leans heavily on a single multi-tenant database layer, ensure you have clear data export or backup escape hatches, and pre-agree on error budget policies for extended recovery. 

The Datadog incident and tooling outages more broadly—be it Prometheus, Grafana, or Jaeger—show that even observability systems can themselves become single points of failure. When monitoring is dark, mean time to knowledge balloons. Distributed teams should architect their observability stack for resilience: separate clusters, out-of-band backup alerts, and possibly vendor diversity can soften the impact when the main platform falters. Real-time user monitoring and adaptive alerting, like those introduced by Datadog and Prometheus, are promising advances—reducing both false positives and alert fatigue—but these should be paired with robust fallback processes. When your main signals go dark, you need escalation pathways and alternate status detection.

Finally, the velocity of updates across the Kubernetes, cloud, and observability spaces brings both opportunity and responsibility. Version 1.30 of Kubernetes and improved cloud-native monitoring tools promise better scaling and deeper insight, but SREs must plan upgrades conservatively, with rigorous soak and rollback plans. Each new feature—a tracing tweak in Jaeger, a dashboarding capability in Grafana—offers leverage for better reliability only if operationalized thoughtfully, with intentional rollout, comprehensive documentation, and real feedback cycles from incident review back into architecture decisions. 

Overall, this week’s incidents reinforce that reliability is not static: it’s a continuous, disciplined effort across the entire stack, encompassing process, tooling, and, above all, a culture of preemption and learning.

## Incidents and Postmortems

Let’s shift gears into incidents and postmortems, where we break down the week’s most impactful reliability events and distill lessons for hardened, production-grade operations. Starting with AWS, July 25th brought another significant outage in the US-West-2 (Oregon) region—a critical hub for cloud workloads. This latest event marks the third major AWS incident in eleven weeks, with each event rooted in a different infrastructural layer: earlier this summer, failures stemmed from cooling hardware and then from a major external network provider; this time, the regional internet boundary layer fell over. The compounded effect is a textbook warning against believing infrastructural risk is siloed; true distributed system resilience depends on holistic scrutiny across physical, network, and logical layers. The chain reaction from AWS’s network instability took down customer-facing platforms including Apple Pay, Reddit, Hulu, DoorDash, and PlayStation Network. For many, business logic and customer identity workflows were disrupted, not just static web content. The 80-minute TTR reflects both the depth of technical complexity and the dependencies revealed as traffic routing, state propagation, and failover logic ground to a halt. For practitioners, this underlines that multi-region resilience is not a luxury. Relying on a single AWS region introduces points of correlated failure that can’t always be predicted or mitigated, even with strong SLAs. If your business runs on AWS or any public cloud, this is a critical prompt to reexamine your deployment architecture: Are your workloads, especially stateful components and critical external integrations, region-agnostic and readily failover capable? Isolation primitives like Route53 for DNS failover, global database replicas, and cross-region service discovery should be kept up to date and regularly tested with game days and chaos scenarios—otherwise you risk “unknown unknowns” surfacing on days like July 25th. Moving to the GitHub Actions runner outage on July 19th: here, a common but still insidious reliability hazard cropped up—certificate expiration. An internal SSL certificate used to secure communications between the Actions runner and core services lapsed, severing a vital operational link. As every experienced SRE knows, while certificate management is routine, it’s often under-automated and under-observed. Expiry not only breaks connectivity but can trigger unexpected cascades, as automation routines, deployment pipelines, or even security policies begin to fail silent or hard. Concretely, the GitHub incident is a dramatic signal that certificate lifecycle management must be automated and observable: integrate auto-renewal via ACME or internal CAs where possible, treat certificates as inventory with centralized expiry tracking, and set up active expiry monitors that alert long before the final day. And remember—testing must cover not just the “happy path” renewal, but all interactions, rollback procedures, and fallback behaviors in complex, multi-service systems. This is how you avoid a one-day certificate creating a one-day outage. Supabase’s authentication service outage, clocking in at 41 minutes, encapsulates a different sort of failure: authentication is the choke point for almost every user action, so auth downtime disproportionately amplifies the customer impact. If your authentication is both the keystone and the single point of failure, downtime knocks every other subsystem offline by proxy. This postmortem lesson is perennial—design for redundancy, especially around stateful, user-centric services. Employing fallback authentication mechanisms, geo-redundant databases, and stateless session issuance wherever possible buys you precious mitigation options. Consider, too, proactive incident response: regular drills for both partial and full auth outage scenarios, and rapid mitigation plans to degrade service gracefully (for example, allowing cached or offline mode logins if appropriate). Turning next to Snowflake, their July 25th outage endured for over seven hours—an eternity in the cloud data warehousing world. The protracted disruption demonstrates the need for both deep technical and operational resilience. Long incidents like this stress test not only technical failover paths such as hot standby and cross-provider replication, but also your human-in-the-loop processes: shift handovers, status communication, incident command rotations, customer outreach. For service owners, this is a sharp reminder to maintain playbooks for extended incidents, not just brief disruptions. Runbooks should cover iterative mitigation steps and canonical communication templates. Technical resilience means regularly exercising backup paths—can you switch queries to a read-only mode, restrict new writes, or shift workload to DR (Disaster Recovery) replicas with minimal manual intervention? Run these scenarios as part of chaos engineering and business continuity exercises. On July 24th, Akamai’s CDN trouble took content offline worldwide for 41 minutes. To many end-users, the CDN is simply “the internet's fast lane” but under the surface, it is an intricate distributed system. Akamai’s outage highlights the risks of single-provider reliance in your content delivery pipeline. RTO and RPO targets fall apart if you lack multi-CDN strategies. Savvy site reliability teams are increasingly looking at DNS-based load balancing, origin failovers, and real-time health checks between multiple CDN providers. This isn’t just for Fortune 500 companies—any organization whose customer experience depends on uptime at global scale benefits from these patterns. Tooling to monitor CDN health, automate failover, and throttle response to “grey failures” can drastically limit the impact surface. GitHub’s Issues and Pull Requests service disruption, lasting over eight hours, has ramifications beyond simple downtime numbers. Collaboration tools underpin developer velocity and are part of the software supply chain now; outages introduce delays, increase merge conflict risks, and create downstream impacts for infrastructure as code, CI/CD, and deployment automation. First, treat third-party platforms as high-value dependencies: prioritize monitoring their status APIs, and implement alerting not just for hard outages but for degraded performance using baseline SLOs. Second, equip your engineering teams with contingency playbooks—offline workflow guidance, alternative repository access, and backup plan protocols for major code freeze situations. Microsoft Azure’s nearly eleven-hour identity service (Entra) outage on July 23 was perhaps the most disruptive event for customers relying on federated IAM and single sign-on. When identity fails, nearly every aspect of multi-tier applications, access controls, and customer API integrations can fall silent. Immediate incident priorities are rerouting authentication traffic, leveraging backup identity providers if available, and keeping customers tightly informed. From an SRE planning perspective, this is another wake-up call for decoupling dependency on any one cloud-provided identity source and building in layers: MFA fallback, local emergency accounts, or even temporary tokenization to sustain a subset of operations in degraded mode. Let’s not overlook the Datadog monitoring platform’s Metrics and Infrastructure outage on July 23rd, resolved after roughly an hour. Best practice here involves running independent parallel observability stacks for critical telemetry: perhaps Datadog plus a self-hosted Prometheus/Grafana pipeline, or rolling backup exporters that can be activated on demand. Redundancy in tooling ensures you don’t fly blind at the very moment you need insight most. Stay vigilant, automate where possible, and always think in production.

## Platform/Cloud Updates

Let’s turn now to the major platform and cloud updates making waves in SRE this week. First up, let’s talk about AWS, because, in light of the recent string of outages—including last week’s high-profile regional failure—they’ve moved to bolster their regional resilience roadmap. Concretely, AWS announced a suite of new architectural investments targeting the so-called “internet boundary” layer—the point where AWS’s internal networks interconnect with external internet providers and their own upstream carriers. Network boundary disruptions have historically proved thorny to mitigate, so AWS has started rolling out both additional border gateway routers and diversified provider peering in critical regions like us-west-2. For SRE teams, that’s immediately relevant: if your deployment patterns are concentrated in a single region, particularly Oregon, now’s the time to assess your blast radius. AWS is also pushing enhancements to its internal alerting for sudden BGP route flaps and upstream peer failures. This will allow for shorter detection-to-mitigation times in cases where internet connectivity begins to degrade, aiming to drain problem traffic from impacted links before users notice. SREs should engage their AWS TAMs to understand how these changes will propagate in their accounts, and whether new region-level health signals from AWS can be integrated into your incident detection flows or playbooks.

Shifting over to Azure, we’re seeing the fruits of Microsoft’s massive investment in Entra, their identity platform. After July’s near 11-hour identity outage, Azure is rolling out advanced identity protection features as part of Entra’s hardening. The new anomaly detection leverages live ML-based baselining to catch outlier authentication patterns, such as spike in failed logins or geo-divergent token requests, and can now—importantly—auto-trigger pre-scripted mitigations. For SREs managing Azure-backed services, this means that certain classes of “identity storms” (for example, botnets or credential stuffing leading to account lockouts at scale) should be intercepted and remediated much faster. Additionally, Azure is revamping their built-in logging for authentication services, so you’ll want to check your monitoring integrations: expect new metrics flowing via Azure Monitor, especially related to authentication attempt latency and error rate heatmaps broken down by identity provider.

Next, let’s dig into Google Cloud’s fresh observability tools—a domain where GCP continues to distinguish itself. The expanded monitoring, logging, and tracing stack is now exposing richer source-level context, especially for distributed services running across multi-cluster Kubernetes. Notably, Google is rolling out “Cross-Cluster Trace Flow,” a feature that reconstructs end-to-end latency and causality chains—even for requests traversing GKE clusters in different regions. From an operational perspective, this means much faster root-cause analysis for region-hopping incidents or degraded microservices whose symptoms were previously lost in the network shuffle. Google’s new logging improvements also allow you to ingest logs into BigQuery with finer retention tuning and more granular field-level security. If you’re instrumenting your own tracing, look for the documentation update on GKE Workload Identity—new service-account scoping should reduce accidental over-permissioning as you delegate trace collection at scale.

On the Kubernetes front, version 1.30 hit general availability this week. Several headline features target reliability and day-two operations. Node management is getting a boost: “Node lifecycle hooks” now allow custom logic to run at defined lifecycle points—before draining, after initialization, and more—enabling safer node upgrades and blue-green deployment automation. Security is strengthened too, with pod sandboxing and more granular runtime policy controls coming into stable. For SREs, perhaps the most impactful is improved support for stateful workloads. The new storage engine APIs enable more predictable volume failover in stateful sets, and there’s tighter integration with CSI drivers for encrypted persistent volumes. If you maintain large Kubernetes clusters, review the deprecation schedule carefully—several legacy APIs and beta features are coming out. Plan for staged upgrades and plenty of node pool canarying before moving production traffic to 1.30.

Cloudflare’s announcement of multi-CDN support is a direct response to the Akamai outage lessons. Their new orchestration layer enables real-time metrics-driven load balancing across multiple CDN providers, including fallback to secondary providers when the primary fails health checks or performance SLAs. Mechanistically, this means your DNS can now dynamically return CDN CNAMEs based not just on geography but also provider health and latency seen by real users. For reliability engineering, this is a significant reduction in exposure to correlated CDN outages. SRE teams with heavy web delivery footprints should evaluate the cost-benefit of onboarding a multi-CDN architecture: configuration management now becomes more complex, but the tradeoff is measurable risk mitigation. Ensure you implement synthetic and RUM-based monitoring across all enabled CDNs for full end-to-end observability.

Zooming out, the takeaways for SRE teams are actionable. First, closely track how your cloud and platform providers are evolving their reliability and security tooling—these updates can directly affect your operational posture, mean time to detect, and mean time to recover. Skipping change reviews or delaying version adoption (especially for Kubernetes or platform identity features) might mean carrying technical risk or missing out on easier mitigations during the next incident. Second, with multi-cloud and multi-region architectures moving from “nice-to-have” to “essential resilience practice,” every team should be re-auditing failover plans, connectivity dependencies, and incident war game playbooks. The past month’s sequence of major outages has made it clear: platform and cloud reliability isn’t static, and neither can your engineering approach be.

## Observability and Tooling

Let’s talk about the recent developments in observability and tooling—areas that have never been more critical given the service disruptions we’ve seen across the industry this past week. We’ll break down not only the features launching in your favorite tools, but also how these capabilities affect day-to-day site reliability practices, and where your incident response and ongoing operational strategies can evolve. First, let’s dig into the enhancements coming out of the Prometheus ecosystem. Adaptive alerting has landed, a feature that intelligently tunes alert thresholds based on historical signal and trend data. This is a meaningful shift from static alerting, which so often contributes to alert fatigue. With the classic model, you might set a fixed CPU load threshold or request error count, but as we’ve all seen—workload patterns change, adoption seasons fluctuate, and baseline “normal” varies widely from service to service. Adaptive alerting learns these patterns, adjusting alert thresholds automatically to avoid unnecessary pages while still surfacing genuinely anomalous conditions. In operational terms, you’ll need to recalibrate not just your alert rules, but also your runbooks and escalation policies—since the context and urgency of each alert might change. If you’re not ready to roll this out across the board, I’d recommend running adaptive and static alerts in parallel for a period, comparing the rate of actionable versus non-actionable alerts. Dashboarding remains the lingua franca for incident and SLO reviews, so any improvements here land directly in the operational workflow. The new dashboard features—such as templated dashboards and more flexible, context-aware visualizations—mean you can more rapidly build and share views that map exactly to what your SREs, developers, or business stakeholders need during an outage or a reliability review. 0, which is crucial for organizations running a blend of metrics, logs, and traces from diverse backends: Prometheus, Loki, Elastic, or proprietary data sources. This is particularly relevant in large or federated environments, where service owners might use different observability stacks. Grafana’s improved data source plugin system makes it less arduous to mix and match, and allows you to keep your “single pane of glass” promise. From an operational readiness perspective, don’t underestimate the value of improved visualization. During a major incident, the ability to overlay application errors, infrastructure health, and user impact metrics in a single, real-time view shortens detection and diagnosis. Schedule time for SREs to experiment with designing dashboards for the most common incident scenarios—like authentication spikes or DB connectivity drops—and update your on-call playbooks to include links to these new dashboards. Let’s talk distributed tracing—Jaeger’s recent enhancements are aiming directly at scaling and visualization pain points we all encounter in production. High-throughput environments, particularly those running microservices or event-driven architectures, have traditionally struggled with the overhead and complexity of trace collection at scale. Jaeger’s improvements have optimized both the trace ingestion pipeline and in-UI visualization. Practically, this enables teams to capture high-fidelity traces—even under heavy load—without incurring prohibitive resource costs. This is particularly relevant during cascading failures or during “brownout” periods when latency spikes across multiple services. Improved visualizations support faster identification of where the bottleneck or error propagation is actually starting—critical when seconds count, as with the multi-hour Snowflake and GitHub outages this week. Here, you’ll want to ensure your sampling strategies are tuned to focus on error paths and slow queries, and revisit how trace IDs are correlated against logs and metrics in your toolchain. Datadog’s newly launched real-time user monitoring feature addresses a long-standing pain point: the time lag between a user-facing issue arising and engineering teams being alerted. Unlike traditional metrics or synthetic monitoring, real-time user monitoring gives you a per-user, per-session lens into degraded experience, surfacing slow transactions, failed interactions, or broken workflows as they happen. There’s operational power here—teams can triangulate between actual user complaints and backend anomalies, prioritizing response to issues that real people are seeing in production. In an environment where both platform outages and tooling disruptions are more common, robust, context-rich observability is a foundational investment in reliability.

## Practical Takeaways

Let’s break down some of the operational lessons and technical mechanisms these incidents are highlighting for SRE practices today.

First, let’s talk about certificate management. The GitHub Actions runner outage, caused by an expired internal SSL certificate, should remind every SRE that certificate expiration is more than just a security risk—it's a direct availability risk. Operationally, treat certificates like other critical resources: use centralized certificate management systems that integrate directly with your configuration management or service mesh tooling, and enforce automation for certificate renewal and deployment. Don't rely solely on calendar reminders—continuous monitoring is key. Push certificate expiry metrics into your observability stack, create dependable alerts, and test your certificate renewal pipelines regularly by running fire drills. After renewal, validate that dependencies—such as build agents, CI/CD runners, egress proxies—actually pick up new certificates seamlessly. This closes a common gap between certificate validity and real-world service availability.

Next, the AWS outage in US-West-2 spells out a recurring theme: locality of failure and the criticality of architectural redundancy. AWS’s three outages in eleven weeks hit different stack layers—hardware, core transit, internet edge—a clear illustration that no single risk model is enough. From an SRE perspective, review your architecture diagrams: is your failover truly regional, or is your critical path single-homed, relying on one provider or one region? Multi-region or multi-cloud strategies come with operational overhead, but they are increasingly necessary relative to the scale of impact we’re seeing from regional outages. Test your regional failover flows at least quarterly. Use disaster recovery (DR) exercises to force failover, and after the drill, look for statefulness and dependencies that did not fail gracefully.

The Snowflake outage is another signal to practice resilience engineering beyond the “happy path.” Seven hours of downtime for a core infrastructure provider is a worst-case event—so run tabletop exercises and chaos experiments specifically against assumed-reliable third-party dependencies. Can you reroute traffic? Can you gracefully degrade? Do you have circuit breakers or backoff logic in your SDKs and API clients to avoid overload during partial recovery? Proactively document these options for operators so response isn’t improvisational in the heat of incident.

Akamai’s CDN disruption reinforces the argument for multi-CDN architectures. Many engineering teams stick with a single CDN for simplicity, but the cost of a 40-minute outage can outweigh the complexity of multi-CDN failover. From a technical perspective, investigate DNS-based load balancing solutions, such as weighted round-robin or geo-aware DNS, to steer traffic between providers. Automate CDN validation checks and configure fallback rules, ensuring static content, API traffic, and other resources can be routed to alternates in real time.

Service monitoring itself is now a dependency, as shown in the Datadog Metrics and Infrastructure event. The operational lesson: observability stacks require redundancy, too. Have a backup telemetrics pipeline—perhaps something as simple as Prometheus pushgateway or a log exporter writing to object storage—that activates in Datadog’s absence. Design internal runbooks so that, if your primary SaaS monitoring provider is down, you can still diagnose, escalate, and respond to critical incidents using secondary or fallback telemetry sources.

The disruptions in collaboration and identity platforms—GitHub Issues, Azure Entra, and Slack—demonstrate that SRE responsibility doesn’t end with your own production stack. Review which business-critical processes depend directly on SaaS vendors. Model “what if” scenarios for every external dependency—think: outage disclosure, alternate workflow, or even redundant provider integrations. For example, with identity outages, have documented contingency processes for authentication bypass, local cache validation, or even manual credential allocation, coupled with controls to rollback to normal as soon as the primary provider recovers. For collaboration services, communicate early and often. Set clear expectations with users, update status pages proactively, and make sure you have alternate communication tools—like SMS or phone trees—for coordination when Slack or GitHub is unavailable.

Across all these incidents, clear, actionable incident communication is vital at every step. Script your user-facing status updates ahead of time, separating what’s known, what’s being investigated, and what mitigation actions users can take. After every major incident, conduct thorough postmortems—not just on your systems, but also on your processes, third-party dependencies, and communications. Feed these learnings back into your oncall training and playbooks.

Lastly, take advantage of platform advances: for instance, leverage adaptive alerting (as introduced in Prometheus), automatic failover features, and improved anomaly detection from vendors. But don’t “set and forget”—continuously review which reliability features you actually have enabled and which require further integration work.

Whenever you read about an outage, don’t just consider, “could that happen to us?”—take concrete steps to inject failure modes into your staging and production environments, and document recovery steps. Reliability isn’t just about robust systems; it’s about constantly evolving operational discipline.
